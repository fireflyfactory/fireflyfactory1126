On the Nuclear Man

Man emerged from the primordial soup of the mediterra, shrugged off the viscera of the organisms not as lucky as he, and sat himself down upon a rock. He watched the sun set on the Pleistocene, stood up, turned towards the land, and then developed intercontinental nuclear arms programs.

It was quite a sensible decision, in his defense, and with quite a sensible imperative: "build a bigger bomb before the other guy bombs you." That thing which he called sense, or more accurately, reason, was the alleged faculty which drove him to make a great deal of rather consequential decisions. Reason was an invention which served him well, up until the very end; if a decision met the criteria to be denoted as reasonable, it would be carried out - preemptive strikes, martial maintenance of public order, various genocides, decimations of armies, et cetera. And if a decision failed to meet the criteria of reason - this criteria never really truly being established, mind you, except for some Herculean attempts by a few fellows during a quite barbaric period which was referred to as the Enlightenment - then it was not carried out, and generally its opposite was enacted. The unfortunate truth of the matter was that reason was rather a recent development relative to the various atomic arms races, and was not truly anything greater than a very thin veneer for man's tumultuous desires. That which man desired was called reasonable, no matter how violent or how base, how contrary to the ideas which he professed as the most near and dear to his heart; and the pursuit of all else but the singular desires of man was called unreasonable, no matter how prudent or necessary.

Man desired to win, that was the problem - he wanted to win very badly. On account of some ridiculous anthropological defect, his monkey brains developed a condition which required power, in a way that expressed itself however appropriate to the individual: in some cases he would become a farmer, alien lord of all cattle in his land, a worker, believing himself indispensable to the cause of humanity - no matter how banal that cause would denote his vocation to be. There were many and sometimes quite miniature attempts at becoming a God-king, something greater than the inconsequential lump of mammalian flesh that man correctly defined himself as. But in the history of God-kings there was never a time as dangerous as the very last. Man figured out by some roundabout scientific explanation involving electrons and nuclei that he was capable of crafting a device that allowed him and his entire species to commit a simultaneous suicide.

Oh, well.

That was that story.
